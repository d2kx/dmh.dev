What Is the Voynich Manuscript?

Preserved at Yale University’s Beinecke Rare Book and Manuscript Library as MS 408, the Voynich Manuscript is an illustrated codex handwritten on early 15th-century vellum (radiocarbon-dated to 1404–1438 CE). Across 227 surviving parchment leaves (~240 pages) and 38,949 words, five scribes wrote in an unfamiliar glyph alphabet alongside hand-painted botanical herbs, astronomical star wheels, Zodiac calendars, bathing pools, and apothecary jars.

Physical evidence on the pages shows the book was painted first and written second: scribes broke their text lines 758 times to flow around pre-existing plant stems, roots, and bathing nymphs. Short labels written directly onto painted roots (such as oror on blood-red Alkanet root or darary on main taproots), storage jars (okol), and Zodiac figures reappear inside the surrounding recipe paragraphs.

A Note on This Research & Where to Look Next

Everything presented in this app is part of our own curiosity-driven exploration of the Voynich Manuscript. Our goal is to make the manuscript’s illustrations, transcriptions, and recurring word structures easy to inspect firsthand and to spark curiosity about one of history’s most fascinating books.

Please treat these readings as an exploratory hypothesis rather than a settled decipherment. Many dedicated historians, paleographers, linguists, and independent researchers have devoted decades of their lives to studying MS 408 in far greater depth. We strongly encourage you to explore their work and the primary archival sources as well:

  • voynich.nu (René Zandbergen) ↗ : The foundational scholarly reference on the manuscript’s history, physical codicology, transcription standards (EVA), and statistical properties.
  • Yale Beinecke Digital Collections (MS 408) ↗ : The official archival record and Public Domain high-resolution digital facsimile at Yale University Library.
  • voynich.ninja Research Forum ↗ : An active international forum where researchers discuss paleography, historical herbals, and ongoing empirical analyses.

Medieval manuscripts are numbered by leaf (folio) rather than modern page numbers: r stands for recto (front side), v stands for verso (back side), and multi-panel foldout sheets include a panel number such as f70v2 or f85r2.

Because the manuscript uses its own handwritten character set, scholars transcribe each glyph into Latin letters using the standard European Voynich Alphabet (EVA) (Zandbergen-Landini ZL3b-n corpus). You can also filter pages by Currier Language A or B, the two distinct spelling and statistical dialects identified across the manuscript’s five scribal hands.

Grammar, Root Lexicon & Section Atlases

How do we test what a Voynich root stem means? We start with short labels written directly onto painted drawings, such as oror beside blood-red Alkanet roots on f102v2 or olkol / okoldy beside painted water pipes and storage jars on f82v and f89r2.

When those same root stems appear inside the multi-line recipe paragraphs, they take on grammatical prefixes and suffixes depending on their role in the sentence: o- introduces a named ingredient, d- / s- marks taking a portion from a source, qo- + -edy marks an active boiling or washing step, and -aiin / -am marks a measured dose or recipe stop.
